Is the Consumer Holding Up?

The consumer is roughly two-thirds of the economy, and the interesting thing about consumers is that what they say and what they do come apart. Sentiment slumps often, sometimes deeply, and sometimes for no recession at all. Real spending almost never falls: it has contracted year over year … times since 1960, and every single one began after the recession had already started. This page computes both tables and prints both rules. Nothing here is a forecast.

Income and the buffer

Spending has to come from somewhere. Real disposable income is what arrives; the saving rate is the cushion between what arrives and what goes out. When the saving rate falls, spending is running ahead of income, which is a thing that can continue for a long time and not forever.

Latest: real disposable income growing … percent year over year while real consumption grows … percent, with the saving rate at … percent. That rate sits in the bottom … percent of every month since 1959. Households saved double digits through the 1960s and 1970s; the buffer today is a fraction of that, which is the honest caveat under every reassuring spending number on this page.

Canada: a survey redesign, drawn rather than hidden

Canadian retail trade comes in two pieces. The old survey ran monthly from 1991 and was terminated in December 2022; its replacement starts in January 2017 and is live. They overlap for six years and disagree by about … percent on the same months, because the survey was redesigned. Both are drawn in full, overlap and all. Splicing them would invent a jump that never happened, and the seam is more informative than a smooth line would be.

What Canadians say, as far as anyone can tell for free

A stated gap: there is no free Canadian equivalent of the Michigan index. The Conference Board of Canada's consumer confidence series is paywalled and is not reproduced here. The closest keyless public survey of Canadian consumers is the Bank of Canada's quarterly expectations survey, which asks about prices rather than confidence. It is a narrower instrument and it only starts in 2014, so the Canadian say-side of this page is genuinely thinner than the American one. That is a fact about the data, not a design choice, and the honest move is to say so rather than to substitute something and imply it is the same thing.

Canadians expect … percent inflation over the coming year, against … percent for Americans on the Michigan survey. Both surveys have run above measured CPI for most of their overlap, which is a well-documented property of asking people about prices and not a sign that either survey is broken.

Expect this card to be busy. Retail sales are published as an advance estimate and revised twice; personal income and consumption are revised every month and benchmarked once a year, when several years of history can move at once. Sentiment is a survey and does not restate. Every refresh is diffed against the one before it, and any change to an already-published value is recorded here rather than silently overwritten.

Sources and construction

United States

  • What they say: University of Michigan consumer sentiment UMCSENT, index 1966-Q1 = 100, from November 1952. The survey ran quarterly until 1977 and monthly from January 1978; the historical quarterly portion also ships separately as UMCSENT1 so the cadence change is visible in the data rather than only asserted in a footnote. Expected inflation is MICH, monthly from 1978.
  • What they do: real personal consumption as the index DPCERA3M086SBEA, monthly from January 1959. The monthly real PCE level series (PCEC96) only begins in 2007, so the index is the deep record; index and level give identical year-over-year arithmetic, and the quarterly chained level PCECC96 reaches 1947-Q1 and ships for context. Retail is RRSFS (real) and RSAFS (nominal), both from 1992.
  • The buffer: real disposable personal income DSPIC96 and the personal saving rate PSAVERT, monthly from 1959.
  • Year-over-year series are computed here from the levels in the same payload; nothing is deflated on this site, since every real series arrives real from its publisher.
  • Recession bands: NBER dates via USREC, from the shared dataset.

Canada

  • The anchor: household final consumption expenditure, chained 2017 dollars, seasonally adjusted at annual rates, StatCan table 36-10-0104-01, vector v62305724, quarterly from 1961-Q1 and live. The vector's English title is verified against the live table on every fetch, so a renumbered vector fails loudly instead of returning someone else's numbers.
  • Retail, both halves: table 20-10-0008-01 (vector v52367097) is inactive, running January 1991 to December 2022; its successor table 20-10-0056-01 (vector v1446859483) starts January 2017 and is current. Both are fetched, both are drawn, and the six-year overlap is shown rather than spliced.
  • What they say: Bank of Canada Canadian Survey of Consumer Expectations via Valet, one-year expectations, past-year perceptions and five-year expectations, quarterly from 2014-Q4. No free Canadian consumer confidence index exists; the Conference Board of Canada's is paywalled and is not reproduced. The gap is stated on the card above rather than papered over.
  • Recession bands: C.D. Howe Institute Business Cycle Council chronology, from the shared dataset.

Plumbing

  • FRED is fetched keyless-first (public CSV), keyed API as fallback; StatCan WDS and Bank of Canada Valet have no keys. A failed fetch carries the stored series forward and says so.
  • Refreshes daily via cron. Sentiment lands twice a month (preliminary and final), retail mid-month, income and consumption near month end, and the Canadian quarterly series on StatCan's and the Bank's own schedules. Most runs verify rather than change; the stamp at the top says when the data last moved.
  • Both rules were tuned once against the canonical record at build time and then frozen. They ship inside the payload and are printed above their tables, because a different rule gives a different table and you should be able to see which one produced these.
  • Series follow the shared econ-core contract used across credit, lending, housing, yield, freight, jobs, debt and diesel.
  • Raw data: /api/data, or the files under /data/.
  • Assembled with Claude (Anthropic). Published surveys and published aggregates, with computed history and both rules printed; no forecasts.
